Can birds of prey save aviation fuel?

Birds of prey — eagles, hawks and falcons — do more than soar at impressive heights. Their elegant and aerodynamic formations can significantly reduce the fuel consumption of aircraft traveling in formation. The principle has been known since the 1970s, but only now are airlines beginning to see how they could benefit from it.

"During the first flight, I knew that something unusual was happening," says aerospace engineer Joachim Voswinckel. He and his team at the German Aerospace Center (DLR) had instrumented an Airbus A380 with sensors to measure the air flows around it as it flew in formation behind another A380. The air flows were not what they expected. Instead of the usual turbulent wake that trails behind an aircraft, the leading plane was generating a vortex of air that the following plane could exploit to reduce drag, saving as much as 10% of the fuel it would otherwise have needed.

A similar phenomenon is used by birds in V-formations. "Birds have known for centuries that flying in formation conserves energy," says Voswinckel. The birds in the rear of the formation ride on the wingtip vortex of the bird in front, which gives them lift and helps them fly with less effort. Now Voswinckel and his team at DLR are trying to find out how to replicate this in aircraft.

But there are complications. In a V-formation, the trailing aircraft needs to be in just the right position to take advantage of the leading plane's wingtip vortex. This is not always easy, especially in turbulent air. "You have to be very precise," says Voswinckel. "If you are even a few meters off, you lose the benefit."

Another challenge is that the trailing aircraft needs to be able to withstand the turbulence created by the leading plane. "The vortex can be quite strong," says Voswinckel. "We need to make sure that the aircraft can handle it." DLR has been conducting experiments with two A380s flying in formation, and so far the results have been promising. "We have shown that it is possible to save fuel by flying in formation," says Voswinckel. "But there is still a long way to go before this becomes a practical option for airlines."

The main obstacle is the difficulty of coordinating the flights of two large aircraft. "In theory, it is simple," says Voswinckel. "In practice, it is very complex." The two aircraft need to be in constant communication, and the pilots need to be able to adjust their positions with great precision. This requires a level of coordination that is not currently possible in commercial aviation.

Despite these challenges, Voswinckel is optimistic. "I believe that formation flying will eventually become a reality in commercial aviation," he says. "The potential fuel savings are too large to ignore." DLR estimates that if all aircraft in European airspace could fly in formation, the total fuel savings would be equivalent to the annual fuel consumption of a small country. "That is a huge amount of fuel," says Voswinckel. "And it would also significantly reduce carbon emissions."

The next step is to conduct more tests with larger formations of aircraft. "We want to see if we can get even more fuel savings by having three or four aircraft flying together," says Voswinckel. "If we can, then the benefits will be even greater." The team is also working on developing software that could automate the process of maintaining the correct position in the formation. "If we can make it automatic, then it will be much easier for pilots," says Voswinckel. "And that will make it more likely that airlines will adopt the technology."
