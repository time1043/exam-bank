#### Text 3

```
Paid media, owned media, and earned media are the three main categories of marketing channels. Paid media refer to advertising and promotional content that a company pays to place in various outlets. Owned media are channels that a company controls, such as its website, blog, and social media accounts. Earned media are publicity or exposure gained through word-of-mouth, reviews, and organic mentions.

The distinction between these categories has become increasingly blurred. Consumers can now create their own "earned" media when they recommend products to friends and family. A positive review on a social media platform can reach thousands of potential customers. This has made earned media one of the most powerful forms of marketing. But it has also created new challenges for companies. Unlike paid and owned media, earned media cannot be directly controlled.

Hijacked media represent a new and troubling phenomenon. This occurs when consumers use the tools of earned media to attack a company or brand. A single negative review can go viral and cause significant damage to a company's reputation. Toyota Motor experienced this firsthand when its recall crisis became the subject of intense social media scrutiny. The company's initial response was slow and inadequate, which only fueled the criticism.

The rise of hijacked media has forced companies to rethink their marketing strategies. They can no longer rely solely on paid and owned media to shape their image. They must also monitor and respond to earned media, including negative commentary. This requires a new set of skills and a willingness to engage with consumers in real time. Companies that fail to adapt risk being overwhelmed by the power of social media.

The lesson from Toyota's experience is clear. Companies must be prepared to respond quickly and transparently to hijacked media. They must listen to their customers and address their concerns. And they must recognize that in the age of social media, every customer has the power to shape public opinion. The companies that thrive will be those that embrace this reality rather than resist it.

It's no surprise that Jennifer Senior's insightful, provocative magazine story about raising children has sparked a firestorm. In her article, Senior argues that having children is a fundamentally different experience than it used to be. She suggests that the joy of parenting is not found in the day-to-day grind, but rather in the broader arc of the relationship. "It's the joy of having children," she writes, "not the joy of being a parent."

Senior's article touches on a sensitive subject. Many parents feel that the experience of raising children is misrepresented by celebrity magazines and popular culture. These publications often portray parenthood as an endless source of happiness and fulfillment. But the reality is more complicated. Many parents struggle with the demands of raising children. They feel exhausted, overwhelmed, and sometimes resentful. These feelings are normal, but they are rarely acknowledged in public.

The portrayal of parenthood in celebrity magazines creates unrealistic expectations. It suggests that good parents are always happy and that children always bring joy. This can make parents who feel otherwise feel guilty and inadequate. Childless individuals, meanwhile, may feel pressured to have children in order to be considered complete. The message conveyed by these magazines is misleading and harmful.

Senior's article is a welcome corrective. She acknowledges the difficulties of parenting while also celebrating its rewards. She suggests that the true joy of raising children lies not in the moment-to-moment experience, but in the long-term relationship with one's children. This is a more nuanced and honest portrayal of parenthood than what is typically found in celebrity magazines.

The impact of Senior's article extends beyond the debate about parenting. It raises important questions about how we construct our understanding of happiness. We often look to others — particularly celebrities — for guidance on how to live. But the images they present are often distorted and unrealistic. We would do well to look inward rather than outward for answers about what makes a good life.

### Part C (10 points)

```
The most valuable things in life are not always the most tangible. While it is easy to measure the worth of material possessions, the value of art, beauty, and human connection is far more difficult to quantify. Art, in particular, plays a unique and essential role in enriching our understanding of life. It offers us new ways of seeing the world, challenging our assumptions and expanding our horizons.

Beauty is everywhere, if we only take the time to notice it. It can be found in a sunset, in a piece of music, in the face of a stranger. Art helps us to recognize and appreciate this beauty. It teaches us to look more closely at the world around us and to find meaning in the everyday.

The role of art in society is often underestimated. In an age that prizes efficiency and productivity, art can seem like a luxury. But it is precisely in such times that art becomes most important. It reminds us that there is more to life than work and profit. It gives us a space to reflect, to feel, and to connect with something larger than ourselves.

Art also serves as a mirror of society. It reflects our values, our fears, and our hopes. Through art, we can understand the concerns and aspirations of different cultures and different historical periods. It provides a bridge between the past and the present, and between different communities.

The value of art lies not only in the objects it produces, but in the experiences it creates. A great painting or a moving piece of music can transform our perception of the world. It can evoke emotions we didn't know we had and inspire thoughts we hadn't considered. This transformative power is what makes art essential to a full and meaningful life.
