In the 2006 film version of The Devil Wears Prada, Miranda Priestly, played by Meryl Streep, scolds her unattractive assistant for imagining that high fashion doesn't affect her. Priestly explains how the deep blue color of the assistant's sweater descended over the years from fashion shows to department stores and finally to the bargain bin in which the poor girl undoubtedly found her garment.

This top-down conception of the fashion business could not be more different from its opposite, that of a democratizing fashion, or what we might call "fast fashion." In the fast fashion era, clothes are cheap and trendy. The industry produces garments at lightning speed. Elizabeth Cline, in her book "Overdressed: The High Cost of Cheap Fashion," argues that mass-market labels have turned fashion into a kind of disposable commodity. These labels encourage consumers to shop for new garments more frequently. The result is a mountain of waste.

In the past, only the elite wore the latest fashions. But fast fashion has democratized style. Now, anyone can buy trendy clothes at a fraction of the price of designer labels. This sounds like a good thing. But Cline argues that it has led to a decline in the quality of clothing. Consumers now buy clothes they will wear only a few times before discarding them.

The fast fashion industry has also had a devastating impact on the environment. The production of cheap, trendy clothing requires enormous amounts of water and energy. The chemicals used in textile dyeing pollute rivers and oceans. When these garments are discarded, they end up in landfills. The fashion industry is one of the most polluting industries in the world.

The problem is that consumers are not aware of the true cost of their clothing. The low prices they pay at the register do not reflect the environmental and social costs of production. This is what makes fast fashion such an indictment of our consumption habits. We are buying clothes we don't need, at prices that don't reflect their true cost, and discarding them after wearing them only a few times.

If the industry is to become more sustainable, consumers need to be more mindful of their purchasing decisions. They need to ask themselves whether they really need a new garment. They should consider buying fewer, higher-quality garments that will last longer. And they should support brands that are committed to environmental responsibility. Only then can we hope to reduce the negative impact of the fashion industry on the planet.

An old saying has it that half of all advertising budgets are wasted, but the trouble is, no one knows which half. In the internet age, at least in theory, this fraction can be much reduced. By watching what people search for, click on and say online, companies can aim "behavioural" ads at those most likely to buy.

In the past couple of weeks, a number of developments have brought this issue to the fore. The "do-not-track" feature on most internet browsers allows users to tell advertisers they do not want to be tracked. Microsoft's Internet Explorer was the first major browser to include a do-not-track feature. But the company has not yet said how it will respond. Google, too, is working on its own do-not-track technology. But the advertising industry has not been happy about this trend.

"The industry is going to be upset," says Bob Liodice, president of the Association of National Advertisers. He argues that setting do-not-track as a default "goes against human nature." If consumers choose not to be tracked, then advertisers will have to find other ways to reach them. This could mean more junk ads, not fewer.

The Federal Trade Commission has been pressing for a do-not-track system. But the commission has limited enforcement power. The advertising industry has been lobbying against such a system. The industry argues that behavioural advertising is beneficial to consumers. It allows advertisers to show consumers ads that are relevant to their interests. Without it, consumers would be bombarded with irrelevant ads.

Microsoft's decision to include a do-not-track feature in its browser was a significant move. But the company has not yet made do-not-track the default setting. This means that users must actively choose to enable the feature. Many privacy advocates argue that do-not-track should be the default setting. If users have to take action to protect their privacy, most will not bother.

The advertising industry has responded by creating its own self-regulatory system. The industry has established a set of principles for online behavioural advertising. These principles require advertisers to inform consumers about their data collection practices and to give consumers the ability to opt out. But critics argue that self-regulation is not enough. They say that binding legislation is needed to protect consumer privacy.

The debate over online privacy is far from over. As technology continues to evolve, new challenges to privacy will emerge. The question is whether the industry can be trusted to regulate itself, or whether government intervention is necessary.
