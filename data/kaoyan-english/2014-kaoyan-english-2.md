```
It's no surprise that Jennifer Senior's insightful, provocative magazine cover story, "I love my children, I hate my life," is arousing much chatter — nothing gets people talking like the suggestion that child rearing is anything less than a completely fulfilling, life-enriching experience. Rather than concluding that children make parents either happy or miserable, Senior suggests we need a new definition of happiness. She argues that we need to re-examine our entire approach to raising children, not just the way we think about whether parents are happy or not.

To find out, she and her team set up a study in which people from different backgrounds were asked to describe their daily activities and how they felt about them. She found that the best predictors of happiness were not material possessions or income, but rather relationships and a sense of purpose. "People who described their lives as meaningful also reported being happy," she says.

For this reason, we should feel liberated to pursue our own definitions of happiness, rather than buying into the conventional wisdom that says we should all be striving for the same thing. For one thing, Senior's research suggests that the things that make us happy are often not the things we think we should want. For another, her work shows that happiness is not a destination but a process — it comes from pursuing goals that are personally meaningful, not from achieving them.

Of course, there are costs to pursuing happiness on your own terms. Senior's research found that people who pursued meaning over happiness were more likely to be stressed and anxious. But they were also more likely to feel that their lives had purpose and direction. In other words, there may be trade-offs between happiness and meaning — but it's not clear that one is always better than the other.

The upshot of all this is that we need to rethink what it means to be happy. We need to recognize that happiness is not a one-size-fits-all proposition, and that different people may find happiness in very different things. We also need to acknowledge that the pursuit of happiness can be messy, complicated, and sometimes painful — but that doesn't mean it's not worth pursuing.
```

```
The concept of man versus machine is at least as old as the industrial revolution, but this phenomenon tends to be most acutely felt during economic downturns and fragile recoveries. And yet, it would be a mistake to think we are right now simply experiencing the painful side of a boom and bust cycle. Certain jobs have gone away for good, outmoded by machines. Above all, we need to think carefully about the kind of society we want to build. Technology is not destiny — it's a tool, and how we use it is up to us. We can choose to harness its power for the benefit of all, or we can allow it to deepen existing inequalities. The choice is ours.
```
