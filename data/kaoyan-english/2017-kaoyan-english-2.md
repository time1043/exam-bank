Though often viewed as a problem for western states, the growing frequency of wildfires is a national concern because of its impact on federal tax dollars, says Professor Max Moritz of UC Berkeley. "I'm surprised we're not doing more about it," he says.

In 2015, the U.S. Forest Service for the first time spent more than half of its $5.5 billion annual budget fighting fires — nearly double the percentage it spent on fires 20 years ago. In effect, fewer federal funds today are going towards the states' fire services, which is why so many local governments and private individuals are looking to take over.

As federal funding for fire suppression has increased, so have the costs. "It's ahead, a huge problem from a public expenditures perspective," says Matt Malone, chief executive of the Forest Service. A new approach might be to shift the focus from suppression to prevention. "We need to think about wildfire in a different way," says Moritz. "We need to focus on the things that we can do to reduce the risk of catastrophic fires." This means investing in forest management, thinning forests, and prescribed burns. It also means changing the way we build in fire-prone areas.

But there is a political obstacle. "The public wants to see the government fighting fires," says Malone. "They don't want to see the government letting fires burn." This creates a perverse incentive: the more money the government spends on fire suppression, the more the public expects it to spend.

A new study by the RAND Corporation found that the federal government spends about $2 billion a year on fire suppression, but that the cost of wildfires to local communities is much higher. "The federal government is picking up the tab for fire suppression, but the costs are being borne by local communities," says the study's author. "We need to find a way to share those costs more fairly."
