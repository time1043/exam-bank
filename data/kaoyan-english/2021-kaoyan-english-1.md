A historian who has always been fascinated by how the images we create shape our understanding of the past has uncovered a surprising trend in Victorian photography. Far from the stern, unsmiling portraits that most people associate with the Victorian era, a significant number of photographs from the period show people looking directly at the camera with expressions that reveal far more personality than convention would suggest.

The collection, which the historian has been assembling for over a decade, challenges the popular perception that Victorians were universally serious and dour. "Hidden Victorians" reveals only a tiny percentage of the photographs portraiture undertaken during the period, but what it shows is remarkable. Many of the images depict people in relaxed, informal poses, smiling and laughing in ways that seem surprisingly modern.

During the 1840s, in the earliest days of photography, portraiture was one of the few genres available to photographers. The technology was new and expensive, and sitting for a photograph was a formal occasion that required patience and endurance. Exposure times were long, often lasting several seconds or more, which meant that subjects had to remain perfectly still throughout the process. This practical constraint is one reason why so many Victorian portraits show people with serious, composed expressions.

The expense varied greatly by the 1860s, and the introduction of the Carte de visite format made photography accessible to a much wider audience. These small, affordable prints could be produced in large quantities and were exchanged between friends and family members. The democratization of photography meant that more people could afford to have their portraits taken, and the results were often more relaxed and informal than the formal studio portraits of earlier decades.

Mark Twain, the great 19th-century American author, loathed sitting for photographs. He complained bitterly about the long exposure times and the discomfort of maintaining a fixed expression for what seemed like an eternity. His famous scowl in photographs, he claimed, was not a reflection of his true personality but rather the result of the physical discomfort he experienced during the sitting process.

The historian's research suggests that the Victorians' relationship with photography was far more complex than popular culture would have us believe. While formal portraiture certainly dominated the medium, there was also a rich tradition of more informal, spontaneous photography that captured the everyday lives and personalities of ordinary people. These hidden Victorians, as the historian calls them, remind us that the past was inhabited by real people with the same range of emotions and expressions as ourselves.
