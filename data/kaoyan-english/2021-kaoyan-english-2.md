For the bigger tech companies, their small acquisitions have brought about big changes in the competitive landscape. We're fairly good at judging people based on first impressions. In a study, psychologist Albert Hadley asked participants to watch a 10-second video clip of a professor. The participants were asked to rate the instructor on a number of dimensions, including competence, warmth, and professionalism. The results showed that participants' ratings were remarkably consistent with the professor's official teaching evaluations, suggesting that first impressions can be surprisingly accurate.

Nalini Ambady's study deals with the impact of rapid first impressions. Ambady found that people could form accurate judgments about others based on very brief exposure. In her research, participants who watched 10-second clips of teachers were able to predict student evaluations with remarkable accuracy. Even when the clips were reduced to just five seconds, the judgments remained fairly accurate. However, when participants were given additional information about the teachers, accuracy dropped. This suggests that more information does not always lead to better judgments.

Another sort of "inaccuracies" participants experienced came from reasoning about a person's typical behavior. People tend to make assumptions about how others will behave based on their past actions, but these assumptions are not always correct. Judi Hall mentions driving to show that intuition can be inaccurate.
