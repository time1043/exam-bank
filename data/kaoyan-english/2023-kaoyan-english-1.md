Scientific papers are the bedrocks of progress in research. Each year researchers publish millions of papers in more than 30,000 journals. The peer review process is supposed to ensure quality and reliability, but there is a growing concern that the system is being exploited through a practice known as "citation manipulation."

Citation manipulation refers to the practice of artificially inflating the number of citations a paper receives, often through agreements between authors or journals to cite each other's work regardless of relevance. This practice has become increasingly common and is considered to be a form of academic misconduct that undermines the integrity of the scientific literature.

The worrisome trend is closely linked to the use of the "impact factor" as a measure of a journal's prestige. The impact factor is calculated based on the average number of citations received by papers published in a particular journal. Journals with higher impact factors are considered more prestigious, which creates a strong incentive for editors and authors to boost their citation counts.

Citation manipulation has been an issue for a long time. In the 1970s, researchers noticed groups of journals that disproportionately cite one another. But the practice has grown more sophisticated in recent years, with some journals engaging in complex schemes to artificially boost their impact factors.

One example of this predatory behavior has been the emergence of so-called "citation cartels," where groups of journals agree to cite each other's articles to increase their respective impact factors. In another form of citation manipulation, some journals publish large numbers of review articles that cite many papers from the same journal, artificially inflating the journal's citation count.

A study by researchers at a Dutch university analyzed more than 100,000 papers published in a single year and found that a significant number of citations appeared to be the result of manipulation rather than genuine scholarly interest. The researchers identified several patterns of suspicious citation behavior, including sudden spikes in citations and clusters of papers from unrelated fields citing the same articles.

The consequences of citation manipulation extend beyond individual journals. When citation counts are artificially inflated, it becomes more difficult for researchers to identify truly influential work. This can mislead funding agencies, hiring committees, and other stakeholders who rely on citation metrics to evaluate research quality.

Several initiatives have been launched to combat citation manipulation. Some journals have adopted policies that limit the number of citations an author can include to papers published in the same journal. Others have implemented algorithms that detect suspicious citation patterns and flag potential cases of manipulation.

However, experts say that more needs to be done. "The current system creates perverse incentives that encourage citation manipulation," says one researcher who studies academic publishing. "We need to move beyond simple metrics like the impact factor and develop more nuanced ways of evaluating research quality."
