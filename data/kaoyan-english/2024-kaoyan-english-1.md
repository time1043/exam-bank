When I started looking into recycling of nails, I was struck by how many old nails have been found around the world, with most dating from the Roman period. A Roman nail is a thing of beauty. It has a square shaft, forged from iron, and a flat head. The four corners of the shaft are often slightly flared, which makes it possible for the nail to hold tightly in place.

There is a good reason why so many Roman nails have been found. When Romans dismantled a building, they would often burn it first to reduce the wooden parts to ash. This made it much easier to recover the iron nails from the wreckage, since the ash could simply be swept away. The nails were then reused in new construction. This practice, known as "burn and recover," was standard Roman building demolition.

It has been estimated that the Romans used approximately 150,000 tons of nails in building construction across their empire. That is a very large amount of iron. And iron was expensive to produce in the Roman world. It required mining ore, transporting it to smelting sites, burning charcoal to heat the furnaces, and then forging the metal into useful forms. The entire process was labor-intensive and time-consuming.

So it made good economic sense to recover and reuse nails. But not all Roman buildings were carefully dismantled. When the Romans left Britain in the 5th century, for example, they left behind an estimated 2,500 tons of nails — enough to fill 40 shipping containers. These nails, left scattered across the landscape, were eventually buried by earth and vegetation, where many remained until they were discovered by archaeologists centuries later.

The Romans were not the only people who valued nails highly. Throughout history, nails have been carefully reused in many cultures. In colonial America, the cost of nails was so high that it was common to burn down abandoned buildings specifically to recover the nails. Early American colonists sometimes even burned their own wooden houses when they moved to a new location, simply to reclaim the nails for use in constructing a new home.

The story of nails reminds us that while technology changes dramatically over time, the basic economics of materials remain remarkably constant. Iron nails may be cheap today, but the principle of recovering and reusing valuable materials is the same principle that drives modern recycling programs. The Romans understood this instinctively. The idea that raising children is a full-time job best handled by one parent, usually the mother, has been the dominant cultural norm in Western societies for centuries. But this is not the way most humans have raised their children throughout history, according to Sarah Hrdy, an evolutionary anthropologist at the University of California, Davis. In her research on hunter-gatherer societies, Hrdy has found that children are raised by a wide network of caregivers — a practice she calls "alloparenting," meaning "other parenting."

For most of human evolution, Hrdy argues, it would have been impossible for a single mother to raise a child to adulthood on her own. Hunter-gatherer societies move frequently in search of food, and the work of gathering food, making tools, and maintaining shelter cannot be done by one person alone. Mothers need help, and they get it from fathers, grandmothers, aunts, uncles, siblings, and even unrelated members of the group.

Hrdy's research is supported by studies of modern hunter-gatherer groups in Africa. Among the Hadza people of Tanzania, for example, children are cared for by multiple people from a very early age. Mothers do the majority of feeding and nursing, but other caregivers — called "alloparents" — regularly hold, carry, play with, and comfort children. This allows mothers to focus on the essential work of food gathering.

Hrdy believes that alloparenting was crucial to human evolution because it allowed mothers to have more children, spaced closer together, than would otherwise be possible. Human children take many years to become self-sufficient, and without help from others, mothers would be limited to one or two children at most. With alloparents, mothers could raise three or four children, which accelerated the growth of human populations.

The implications of Hrdy's research extend far beyond anthropological theory. In many modern societies, parents are expected to raise children with little or no help from anyone outside the immediate family. This can lead to enormous stress, particularly for single parents or dual-income couples. Some governments are beginning to recognize this problem.

In Germany, for instance, the government has been investing in publicly funded nurseries and childcare facilities, partly in response to the growing recognition that the nuclear family model is not sufficient for the demands of modern life. Germany's fertility rate had been declining for decades, but in recent years it has risen slightly, which some demographers attribute in part to the expansion of affordable childcare.

Hrdy sees this as a step in the right direction. Humans evolved to raise children cooperatively, she argues, and societies that fail to provide the social structures that support cooperative childrearing will struggle with declining birth rates and the social problems that come with them.

The Chesapeake Bay, the largest estuary in North America, has been at the center of one of the most significant legal battles over environmental regulation in the United States. The dispute revolves around a seemingly simple question: What counts as a navigable waterway under the Clean Water Act?

The Clean Water Act, passed in 1972, gives the federal government authority to regulate pollution in "navigable waters" of the United States. For decades, the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) interpreted this term broadly to include not just major rivers and lakes, but also wetlands, streams, and other water bodies that were connected to them. The logic was simple: pollutants dumped into a small stream would eventually flow into larger rivers and then into the ocean, so all connected waterways deserved protection.

This broad interpretation was challenged in a 2006 Supreme Court case called Rapanos v. United States. The case involved John Rapanos, a Michigan landowner who had filled in wetlands on his property without a permit from the Army Corps of Engineers. The Supreme Court issued a split decision, with no clear majority opinion. However, Justice Antonin Scalia, writing for a plurality of four justices, argued that the Clean Water Act should apply only to water bodies that have a continuous surface connection to navigable waters — meaning they can actually be physically reached by a boat.

Justice Anthony Kennedy, writing separately, proposed a different test. He argued that the Clean Water Act should apply to wetlands that have a "significant nexus" to navigable waters — meaning they have a significant effect on the chemical, physical, or biological integrity of those waters. Kennedy's test was more flexible than Scalia's, but it also introduced significant uncertainty, because it required case-by-case analysis of whether a particular wetland had such a connection.

In practice, Kennedy's test became the controlling standard, and it was adopted by the EPA and the Army Corps of Engineers. But it proved difficult to apply consistently. Developers and landowners complained that it was impossible to know in advance whether a particular piece of land contained protected wetlands, because the "significant nexus" test required extensive scientific analysis.

The Supreme Court addressed the issue again in 2023, in a case called Sackett v. EPA. Michael Sackett, an Idaho homeowner, had been fined for filling in wetlands near Priest Lake, a navigable waterway. The Court, in a 5-4 decision, ruled that the Clean Water Act applies only to wetlands that have a "continuous surface connection" to navigable waters — effectively adopting Scalia's narrower test from Rapanos.

The decision was celebrated by property rights advocates, who argued that the EPA had been using the Clean Water Act to regulate land that had nothing to do with actual waterways. Environmental groups, however, warned that the ruling could leave millions of acres of wetlands without federal protection, threatening water quality and wildlife habitats across the country.

The broader significance of the Sackett decision lies in its potential to reshape the scope of federal environmental regulation. By narrowing the definition of "navigable waters," the Court has limited the reach of one of the most important environmental laws in American history. And it has raised a fundamental question about how much power the federal government should have to regulate private land in the name of environmental protection.
